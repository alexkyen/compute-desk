Section 03 of the guide

What you actually pay.

The hourly price is where the bill starts. What you pay for each useful hour depends on who you buy from, how you buy, and how much of the capacity you actually use.

The short version

  • The same GPU sells for very different prices. In mid-October 2026, an H100 rented on demand listed from $1.33 to $12.29 an hour, while renters actually paid $2.78 on average. Seller type and rental type explain most of the gap.
  • Idle capacity raises the real price. A reserved GPU costs the same whether it is busy or idle, so what matters is the cost of each hour you actually use.
  • Reserve a GPU only if you will use it often enough. The test is simple: it must be busy for a larger share of hours than the reserved rate divided by the on-demand rate.

02What idle capacity costs

Reserved GPUs cost less per hour, but you pay for them every hour. On-demand capacity costs more, but only when work arrives. At $2.00 reserved and $6.00 on demand, a GPU has to be busy more than 33.3% of the time to be worth reserving. Reserve it for 100 hours and you pay $200; rent it for the 50 hours you need and you pay $300.

The same test applies to every additional GPU. Figure 2 sorts a period’s hours from busiest to quietest, so you can see how often each reserved GPU would actually work.

03Ask the seller

A quoted price is incomplete until these are answered. Each seller’s file in Figure 1 adds the question that matters most for that seller.

  1. What exactly is this price for? Region, machine shape, interconnect, term, and start date. Two prices for the same GPU are only comparable when these match.
  2. Is the capacity committed, or only quota? What is energized and orderable today at this rate, and what depends on a future site or delivery date?
  3. What sits outside the hourly rate? Storage, data transfer, support, minimum terms, and prepayment.
  4. Can the commitment move with demand? A check-in to adjust committed volume limits the cost of reserving too much. True-up and true-down

Assumptions, limits, and sources

Every price is a public price, checked on 18 October 2026. Each one links to the seller’s own price page or price API, and its note gives the raw instance price. A per-GPU price is the instance price divided by its GPU count. Where a seller sells a GPU without publishing a price, the board says “price on request” rather than estimating one. Recheck the source before citing a value: list prices move, and large buyers negotiate well below them.

The index is not an offer. Ornn’s Compute Price Index is the volume-weighted average of on-demand rental prices actually paid across its contributing providers, settled daily for four GPUs. It shows where trades clear relative to list prices. Reserved and long-term contracts are outside it, and Ornn discloses that its affiliates trade physical compute.

Switch accelerators before ranking sellers. A seller’s position changes with product, rental type, region, and term. Shortlist within the configuration you are negotiating rather than drawing a provider-wide conclusion from one price.

The commitment model is a newsvendor problem. Reserve a GPU when its expected utilization is at least the reserved rate divided by the on-demand rate. The cost curve often has a broad minimum, so getting the size slightly wrong may matter less than duration, delivery, and counterparty risk, which the model does not price.

Limits. The model assumes the demand distribution repeats, on-demand capacity is always available, rates are flat, and GPU-hours are interchangeable. It omits growth, minimum billing increments, placement, performance, and outages. Imported observations keep their distribution but lose their time order.
