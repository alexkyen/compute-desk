Buying AI compute.

Everyone quotes the hourly price of a GPU. What you actually pay depends on what sits behind that number: the hardware, the network, how much of it you use, and what the contract promises.

The short version

The same GPU listed for anywhere from $1.33 to $12.29 an hour in mid-October 2026, a more than nine-fold spread (Figure 1). The sticker price is the start. Four things decide what you really pay:

  • Memory decides what fits. A 70-billion-parameter model needs about 140 GB for its weights alone, more than one H100 holds.
  • The network decides how long you wait. If GPUs spend 3 of every 13 seconds swapping results, almost a quarter of the rented time is waiting.
  • Utilization decides the real price. A $3 GPU that works half the time costs $6 per useful hour.
  • The contract decides who pays for failures. The same outage can earn a six-figure credit or nothing, depending on what “uptime” measures.

01What you rent

A GPU is two things packaged together: chips that do the math, and memory that holds the model while it works. Memory decides what fits. At 16-bit precision every parameter takes 2 bytes, so a 70-billion-parameter model needs about 140 GB before it does anything at all.

Nobody rents one chip in isolation, though. GPUs come in trays, trays fill racks, and racks are wired into clusters. Each step up adds power, cooling, and connections, and each one shows up somewhere in the price.

02Where the time goes

Large training jobs split the work across many GPUs. On every step, each GPU computes its share, then all of them exchange results before anyone can move on. Some of that exchange overlaps with computing. The rest is time when expensive chips sit and wait.

The network sets how long that wait lasts. Less bandwidth, or one failed switch, stretches every step, and the meter keeps running.

03What you actually pay

A rented GPU costs the same whether it is busy or idle. At $3 an hour, a GPU that does useful work half the time really costs $6 per useful hour.

Reserving capacity lowers the hourly rate, but you pay for every hour. On-demand capacity costs more, but only when you use it. So the question for each extra GPU is whether you will need it often enough. With reserved GPUs at $2 and on-demand at $6, the answer is yes if it is busy more than a third of the time.

04What the contract covers

“99.9% uptime” sounds precise. It isn’t until you know what is being measured. If uptime means the provider’s platform is responding, individual GPUs can fail all month without breaking the promise. If it means GPU-hours actually delivered, the same failures add up.

Before signing, ask three questions: what unit is measured, which failures count, and what the credit is a percentage of. Credits usually offset future bills and rarely cover the cost of lost work.
