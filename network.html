Section 02 of the guide

Where the time goes.

Large jobs spread across many GPUs, and on every step they stop to exchange results. The network decides how long that takes, how much equipment it needs, and what stops working when a switch fails.

The short version

  • Where a job runs matters before how fast the network is. Inside one server, GPUs talk over NVLink at hundreds of gigabytes a second. Across the cluster, every message crosses switches at a small fraction of that.
  • Thinner uplinks stretch every step. With half the capacity between leaf and spine switches, a cluster-wide job waits twice as long to exchange results, and each useful hour costs more.
  • The layout trades flexibility for equipment. Rails need fewer switches for the traffic training expects, but a failed rail switch touches every server, and other messages take a detour.

01The wait in every step

In distributed training, every GPU computes its share of an update, then all of them combine their results in a step called all-reduce. Some of that exchange overlaps with computing; the rest adds to every step while the chips wait.

How long it takes depends on which connections the job uses. Figure 1 runs one step for a job that fits in one server, one group of servers, or the whole cluster, on the layout chosen in Figure 2.

02How the cluster is wired

A fat-tree routes messages through local leaf switches and shared spine switches, so any GPU can reach any other. A rail layout instead wires matching GPU positions across servers to the same switch. Rails need less equipment for the traffic training expects, but messages between different positions take a detour inside a server, and a failed switch takes out one position everywhere.

04Ask the supplier

A protocol name and a line rate are where the conversation starts. These questions get to what the network will actually do for your jobs.

  1. What do you measure, not what is the line rate? Which fabric and NIC generation, and what NCCL all-reduce bus bandwidth do you see at my job size? Enter it in your scenario
  2. What is the oversubscription, leaf to spine? And can your scheduler keep my job inside one leaf group or one rail? Figure 1
  3. Fat-tree or rails? Show me the topology diagram and the physical port map, and tell me exactly which nodes, rails, and jobs stop when one switch fails. Figure 2
  4. If it is rails, is the scheduler rail-aware? What are your measured all-to-all numbers, not just all-reduce? Figure 2
  5. Walk me down the row. Which rack holds the spines, how many fibers are in the tray at my ratio, and what kind of cable is each link? Figure 3
All-reduce
The step where every GPU combines its part of an update with all the others, so each ends up with the same result.
Oversubscription
The ratio of capacity below a leaf switch to capacity above it. At 2:1, traffic leaving the group shares half as much bandwidth.
Fat-tree
Leaf switches for each group of servers, joined by shared spine switches, so any GPU can reach any other.
Rails
One switch per GPU position, linking that position across every server. Efficient for matched traffic; other traffic detours inside a server.

Assumptions, limits, and sources

The step model. One FP16 copy of every gradient moves through a ring all-reduce: exchange time = 2 × gradient size × (GPUs − 1) ÷ (GPUs × bandwidth). The part not hidden by computing is added to the compute-only step, and the quoted price is divided by the share of compute throughput kept. It is a sensitivity calculation, not a benchmark; it leaves out sharding, topology-aware algorithms, pipeline bubbles, congestion, storage, and failures.

Bandwidth. 810 GB/s inside one eight-GPU server; 400 Gb/s (50 GB/s) per GPU outside it, times the protocol’s assumed efficiency, divided by the oversubscription ratio when a fat-tree job crosses the spine. With rails, the all-reduce stays on each GPU’s rail, so uplink sharing does not apply. These are scenario assumptions, not a specification for every H100 system; replace them with measured bus bandwidth.

Protocol labels do not establish delivered performance. InfiniBand and RoCE can run the same wire rate and both carry RDMA. What they deliver depends on NIC and switch generation, topology, congestion control, tuning, and job size.

The parts estimate. A two-tier Clos rule for the fat-tree (half of each switch’s ports face down) and one switch group per rail for rails, multiplied by the number of planes. When a rail needs more than one switch group, links between those groups are not counted. It exposes quote assumptions; it does not replace the supplier’s port map.

Inference is different. Single-server serving may not need a training-grade fabric. Large mixture-of-experts models, split prefill and decode, or models sharded across servers can create heavy traffic between servers; size the network from the serving design and its measured communication.
