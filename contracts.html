Section 04 of the guide

What the contract covers.

“99.9% uptime” means little until you know what it measures, which failures count, and what you get back. Apply different terms to the same month of failures, then work through the fourteen clauses that decide what the promise is worth.

The short version

  • What counts as “unavailable” decides whether you are owed anything. A provider’s API can keep answering while individual GPUs fail. Measured that way, a month that lost thousands of GPU-hours still reads 100%.
  • A credit is a slice of your bill, not of your loss. It is a percentage of monthly fees, usually offsets future invoices, and is often your only remedy.
  • The strongest protections are not credits. Acceptance tests, a replacement deadline, reserved spares, and an exit after repeated breaches do more than any credit tier.

01What counts as unavailable

A provider’s control platform can keep responding while individual machines are down. If the contract defines availability by the platform, those failures never count. If it measures delivered GPU-hours, every failed server adds up.

02What a credit is worth

Even when a failure counts, the remedy is a percentage of the monthly bill, set by a tier schedule. It is usually applied to future invoices rather than paid in cash, and the contract often makes it your only remedy. Compare it with what the lost time cost you.

Delivery damagesDelivery · clause 1“Commercially reasonable efforts” toward a target date0.5% of the prepay per day late; walk away with a full refund past 45 days—

The draft says: “Commercially reasonable efforts” toward a target date. You ask for: 0.5% of the prepay per day late; walk away with a full refund past 45 days.

Why it matters. The draft states a target date without an explicit delivery credit or delay-based exit right.

Provider shall use commercially reasonable efforts to make make the Committed Capacity available by the target date. If Ready-for-Service is delayed beyond the target date, Provider will credit Customer 0.5% of the Initial Payment per day of delay; if the delay exceeds forty-five (45) days, Customer may terminate this Order Form with a full refund of the Initial Payment.

Effect of the change. The proposed text ties a missed delivery date to a credit and an eventual exit right. Both the trigger and the remedy need to be defined.

How the figure is set. 1.5 × monthly fees: a 45-day slip, with committed spend and no service or remedy.

Acceptance testingDelivery · clause 1Ready for service on the provider’s own validationBurn-in and acceptance tests to your spec; billing starts at your sign-off—

The draft says: Ready for service on the provider’s own validation. You ask for: Burn-in and acceptance tests to your spec; billing starts at your sign-off.

Why it matters. The draft lets the provider decide when capacity is ready and start billing on its own validation.

Capacity is Ready for Service upon Provider's completion of its standard validation procedures. completion of burn-in and acceptance testing — sustained multi-day load, interconnect at specified bandwidth, HBM and ECC error rates within specified thresholds — and Customer's written sign-off.

Effect of the change. The proposed acceptance test connects billing to agreed performance criteria and your sign-off.

How the figure is set. 1 × monthly fees: a first month billed on a cluster that fails your spec.

True-up and true-downMoney · clause 2Committed volume can never be reduced±15% at months 9 and 18, at the committed rate—

The draft says: Committed volume can never be reduced. You ask for: ±15% at months 9 and 18, at the committed rate.

Why it matters. The draft fixes committed volume for the whole term. A later fall in demand does not reduce what you owe.

Committed volume may not be reduced during the Committed Term. Committed volume may be adjusted upward or downward by up to fifteen percent (15%) at months nine (9) and eighteen (18), at the Committed Rate (true-up / true-down).

Effect of the change. The checkpoints allow a limited reduction in committed volume. Their timing and size decide how much demand risk remains with you.

How the figure is set. 0.15 × monthly fees × the 15 months after month 9: demand falls 15% at the first checkpoint, and the draft makes you pay for it anyway.

Energy pass-throughMoney · clause 3Power-cost increases pass through on 30 days’ notice, uncappedCapped at 5% per contract year, with auditable documentation—

The draft says: Power-cost increases pass through on 30 days’ notice, uncapped. You ask for: Capped at 5% per contract year, with auditable documentation.

Why it matters. A pass-through can raise the effective rate even when the quoted base rate is fixed.

The Committed Rate is fixed for the Committed Term, except that Provider may pass through documented increases in power and facility input costs on thirty (30) days' notice, not to exceed five percent (5%) in any contract year, with supporting documentation subject to Customer audit.

Effect of the change. The cap limits annual increases and gives you access to the supporting documents. It still allows some price movement.

How the figure is set. 0.05 × 12 × monthly fees: one year of increases at the 5% cap you ask for, as a floor on the uncapped exposure.

Prepay refundsMoney · clause 3The initial payment is non-refundable unless you terminate for causeRefundable pro rata on any termination—

The draft says: The initial payment is non-refundable unless you terminate for cause. You ask for: Refundable pro rata on any termination.

Why it matters. Refund rights decide how much prepaid cash comes back when the agreement ends.

The Initial Payment is non-refundable except upon Customer's termination for cause refundable pro-rata upon any termination of this Order Form.

Effect of the change. Unused prepaid amounts come back on termination. The effect depends on the refund calculation and the other exit terms.

How the figure is set. The prepay itself: cash forfeited under the draft on any termination that is not for your cause.

Definition of “available”Availability · clause 4“Available” means the platform API answersAvailability measured per committed GPU-hour, at the node level—

The draft says: “Available” means the platform API answers. You ask for: Availability measured per committed GPU-hour, at the node level.

Why it matters. A platform-only definition can leave out individual machine failures while the provider’s API stays reachable. Figure 1 compares the two measurements.

4.1 "Monthly Uptime Percentage" means the percentage of minutes in a calendar month during which the Service is Available. The Service is "Available" when Customer is able to access the Provider platform API. a Committed GPU-hour is delivered by a node that is operational and performing to specification; a GPU-hour is Unavailable if its node is failed, degraded below specified interconnect bandwidth or error thresholds, or withdrawn from service. Monthly Uptime is computed over Committed GPU-hours.

Effect of the change. The measured unit becomes delivered GPU-hours, which changes which incidents can trigger a credit.

How the figure is set. The credits a bad year would earn when GPU-hours are measured, compared with none when only the platform is.

ExclusionsAvailability · clause 4Maintenance uncapped; node failures excluded by nameAt most 8 hours of maintenance a month on 7 days’ notice; the node exclusion struck—

The draft says: Maintenance uncapped; node failures excluded by name. You ask for: At most 8 hours of maintenance a month on 7 days’ notice; the node exclusion struck.

Why it matters. The draft excludes individual node and component failures even when they reduce the capacity you can use.

4.2 Excluded from the calculation: scheduled maintenance, not to exceed eight (8) hours per month on seven (7) days' notice; events beyond Provider's reasonable control; failures of individual nodes, GPUs, or components where the platform remains accessible; and suspensions under Clause 6.

Effect of the change. Scheduled maintenance gets a limit, and the named exclusion for individual-node failures goes away.

How the figure is set. (16 ÷ 730) × monthly fees × 12: uncapped maintenance at 24 hours a month against the 8 you ask for, over a year.

Replacement time and hot sparesAvailability · clause 4Not in the draftFailed nodes replaced within 6 business hours from a 2% hot-spare pool—

The draft says: Nothing. You ask for: Failed nodes replaced within 6 business hours from a 2% hot-spare pool.

Why it matters. The draft sets no deadline for replacing a failed node and reserves no spares for your cluster.

4.5 Provider will replace any failed node within six (6) business hours from a hot-spare pool maintained at no less than two percent (2%) of cluster size (time-to-replace).

Effect of the change. A replacement deadline and reserved spares bring capacity back. Check when the clock starts, which hours it covers, and what happens if the deadline is missed.

How the figure is set. 0.25 × monthly fees: a quarter-month of capacity protected across the term.

The draft says: Nothing. You ask for: Three breaches in six months lets you terminate, with no cure period and a pro-rata refund.

Why it matters. Credits for individual bad months never add up to a way out.

4.6 If Monthly Uptime breaches the Committed Level in any three (3) months within a rolling six (6) month window (chronic breach), Customer may terminate for cause without cure period, with pro-rata refund of the Initial Payment.

Effect of the change. A pattern of failures becomes grounds to leave. It depends on which incidents the definition of “available” counts.

How the figure is set. Optionality, deliberately left unnumbered.

Cure periodExit · clause 6Every breach gets 30 days’ notice and cureA 15-day cure for availability; repeated breaches skip the cure—

The draft says: Every breach gets 30 days’ notice and cure. You ask for: A 15-day cure for availability; repeated breaches skip the cure.

Why it matters. The time allowed to fix a breach decides when your other remedies become available.

Either party may terminate for material breach not cured within thirty (30) days of written notice, except that chronic breach under Clause 4.6 requires no cure period and availability breaches carry a fifteen (15) day cure.

Effect of the change. The availability cure period gets shorter, and repeated breaches are treated separately.

How the figure is set. 1 × monthly fees: one month of exposure before any remedy takes effect.

Liability cap and sole remedyMoney · clause 6Liability capped at 3 months’ fees; credits are the sole and exclusive remedyA 12-month cap, with confidentiality, data protection, and gross negligence carved out—

The draft says: Liability capped at 3 months’ fees; credits are the sole and exclusive remedy. You ask for: A 12-month cap, with confidentiality, data protection, and gross negligence carved out.

Why it matters. The cap, the sole-remedy wording, limits on credits, and the claim deadline can each limit what you recover.

PROVIDER'S AGGREGATE LIABILITY UNDER THIS AGREEMENT IS CAPPED AT FEES PAID IN THE THREE (3) MONTHS TWELVE (12) MONTHS PRECEDING THE CLAIM, AND SERVICE CREDITS ARE CUSTOMER'S SOLE AND EXCLUSIVE REMEDY FOR ANY FAILURE OF AVAILABILITY. THE CAP DOES NOT APPLY TO BREACHES OF CONFIDENTIALITY OR DATA PROTECTION, OR TO PROVIDER'S GROSS NEGLIGENCE OR WILLFUL MISCONDUCT.

4.3 If Monthly Uptime falls below the Committed Level of %, Customer's sole and exclusive remedy is a Service Credit per the Schedule below, applied against future invoices only, provided Customer submits a written claim with supporting logs within thirty (30) days of the incident. Credits are calculated automatically from Provider telemetry, which Customer may audit on request.

Effect of the change. The liability cap and sole-remedy language stay in place; a wider cap, carve-outs, or an added exit need separate review with counsel. Automatic credits from auditable telemetry remove the claim paperwork, though the measurement and dispute process still need to be defined.

Read together: sole-remedy language and the liability cap can sharply limit what any availability promise is worth.

How the figure is set. 9 × monthly fees: on a catastrophic claim, the difference between the 12-month cap you ask for and the draft’s 3-month cap.

The draft says: Supplier shortages and delays count as force majeure. You ask for: Supply-chain delays do not excuse delivery under clause 1.

Why it matters. The draft lists supplier shortages and delays among the events that excuse performance.

04Ask the supplier

Four questions that test the promise before you sign. Each one points to the clause it affects.

  1. Define “unavailable” for me. Does it include a single node down, or degraded interconnect performance, or only full loss of the API? What was your fleet’s node-level annualized failure rate last quarter? Definition of “available” · Exclusions
  2. How is it measured and claimed? Monthly or annually? Read me the exclusions list. Are credits automatic, or do I file a claim with logs within 30 days? Exclusions · Liability cap and sole remedy
  3. What happens when a node fails? What is your committed time to replace it, do you hold hot spares against my cluster, and can I terminate after repeated breaches? Replacement time · Exit after repeated breaches
  4. When does billing start? At your own validation, or when the cluster passes my acceptance tests? Acceptance testing
Time to replace
A contractual maximum on how long a failed node can stay out of service.
Hot-spare pool
Standby nodes reserved for your cluster, so a failure is swapped from your own spares instead of waiting on the general fleet.
Burn-in and acceptance testing
Stress tests of memory, NVLink, and the network fabric, with pass criteria agreed before billing starts.
Chronic breach
An exit trigger keyed to a pattern, such as three breaches in six months, rather than one severe incident.

Assumptions, limits, and sources

The definition of “unavailable” decides which incidents count. Platform- or region-level definitions can exclude node, GPU, memory, and network-component failures while the API stays reachable. Meta’s Llama 3 team reported 419 unexpected interruptions during 54 days of training on 16,384 H100s, with hardware failures at the node level; Modal has described recurring machine-level faults across a multi-cloud GPU pool.

The measurement window changes the result. A one-hour outage is about 99.86% availability in a 730-hour month, but about 99.989% over a year. Specify the window, the data source, the level of aggregation, and how claims work, not just the headline percentage.

Credit recovery depends on what is measured. A total platform outage may trigger a tier while recurring node failures never register under a platform-only definition. Credits usually reduce future fees rather than being paid in cash, which makes them worth less when the relationship is ending.

Operational commitments complement credits. Replacement deadlines, dedicated spares, burn-in and acceptance testing, telemetry access, and termination rights deal with failures directly. Review them alongside the availability percentage and credit schedule.

The model. A 730-hour month; the deal’s rate and capacity; credits as a tier percentage of monthly fees; failures spread across the month. Tier schedules are a representative composite.
