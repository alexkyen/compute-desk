Physical-design diligence

The topology diagram becomes a placement and port map. A supplier's logical leaf and spine tiers must resolve to physical switches, ports, cable assemblies, and pathways. This illustration places leaves at top of rack and spines at end of row; other designs use middle-of-row, centralized, or distributed placement. Ask for both diagrams and reconcile them.

Distance and line rate determine media. Short links may use passive copper; longer or faster links may require AEC, AOC, or optical assemblies. A logical edge may map to multiple lanes or cables, and the cost depends on the actual bill of materials. At 2:1, this illustration removes half the leaf-to-spine capacity and associated assemblies. Model the communication sensitivity →

Rails change both cost and failure shape. In the single-plane illustration, removing a rail switch affects the corresponding path across attached nodes. Redundant planes, routing, and collective configuration determine whether jobs stall or continue at reduced bandwidth. Configure the topology estimate →

Ask the supplierWalk me down the row: which rack holds the spines, how many fibers are in the tray at my oversubscription ratio — and when one rail switch dies, which port goes dark on which racks, and which of my jobs stall?

The toy pod keeps the wiring readable: eight racks, four spines, one fiber per graph edge, counts in the dozens. Real rows run longer, real pods span many rows, and real trays carry thousands of fibers. The geometry of the argument — copper inside the rack, light between racks, rails as stripes down the row — is what scales; the numbers are not.
